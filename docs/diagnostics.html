Diagnostics Research | Ayel L. R. Batac

Research on diagnostic innovation by Ayel L. R. Batac, focusing on self-testing technologies, infectious disease detection, and expanding equitable access to diagnostics.

DIAGNOSTICS

\(~\)

Diagnostics are fundamental to effective public health systems. Timely and accurate testing helps identify disease early and informs treatment. Test results also provide valuable information about patterns of infection, helping public health systems respond to emerging threats. Yet many health systems continue to rely heavily on conventional facility-based testing, which can limit access for people who cannot easily visit a clinic or hospital.

For sexually transmitted infections (STIs), delayed or missed diagnoses can leave infections untreated, increasing the risk of further transmission and complications. Syphilis, for example, has resurged globally despite the availability of effective testing and curative treatment. Because early infection may go unnoticed, access to testing is particularly important. Stigma can discourage people from seeking STI services, while visiting a clinic may be impractical when travel is costly or requires time away from work.

Expanding access requires diagnostic approaches that reduce reliance on facility-based testing without compromising diagnostic accuracy or access to treatment and follow-up care.

Decentralising Diagnostics Through Self-Testing

My research examines how diagnostic testing can be made more accessible beyond conventional healthcare facilities. One approach is self-testing, which allows individuals to perform diagnostic tests and interpret the results themselves, often at home.

In August 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration authorised the first over-the-counter syphilis self-test in the United States. The test uses a finger-prick blood sample to detect antibodies to Treponema pallidum, with results available in approximately 15 minutes. Evidence suggests that quality-assured syphilis self-tests can provide good diagnostic accuracy and are generally acceptable to users. However, because these antibodies can persist after treatment, a positive result does not necessarily indicate an active infection.

For people who find clinic visits difficult or are concerned about stigma, self-testing can provide a more private way to access testing. A positive result still requires further assessment by a healthcare professional to determine whether additional testing or treatment is needed.

Expanding Access to STI Testing

Studies of HIV self-testing have shown that offering people the option to test themselves can increase testing rates compared with conventional services alone. Research on syphilis self-testing has produced similar findings, suggesting that it could help reach people who might otherwise miss out on testing.

Community-based distribution can extend access to self-testing beyond conventional healthcare facilities, building on experience from existing HIV self-testing programmes. Digital tools may provide additional support by helping people understand their results and find appropriate care when needed.

People who receive a positive self-test result still need access to confirmatory testing to determine whether they have an active infection and require treatment. Without this connection to healthcare services, increased testing may not translate into earlier treatment or better health outcomes.

Implementation and Health System Integration

Decentralising STI testing does not eliminate the need for integration with healthcare services. Individuals who receive a positive syphilis self-test result still require further clinical assessment and confirmatory testing to determine whether treatment is needed. Without clear referral pathways connecting self-testing to appropriate care, increased testing may have limited impact on disease transmission and health outcomes.

These referral pathways must also be accessible to the communities they are intended to serve. Community engagement can help identify barriers to follow-up care and ensure that referral processes reflect local needs. Integrating self-testing into existing health systems is therefore essential to maintaining continuity of care, even as testing moves beyond conventional healthcare facilities.

Contribution to Public Health

When self-testing reaches people who might otherwise remain untested, it creates opportunities to identify STIs earlier. If those results lead to appropriate treatment, earlier diagnosis may help prevent complications and reduce further transmission. The public health benefit therefore depends on more than simply increasing the number of tests performed.

Research on how self-testing works in practice can help public health programmes determine where these approaches add value to existing services, particularly for communities underserved by conventional facility-based testing. Decentralised testing may also contribute to disease surveillance, although this requires reliable ways to capture relevant testing information while protecting individual privacy. Such evidence can inform the wider adoption of self-testing within public health systems.

Featured Publication

Syphilis self-testing and implications for syphilis control and prevention

This Editor’s Pick article in the Journal of Clinical Microbiology examines how syphilis self-testing could expand diagnostic access for people underserved by conventional facility-based services. Drawing on emerging evidence and lessons from HIV self-testing, the article explores how community-based approaches can support wider adoption while maintaining clear referral pathways for confirmatory testing and treatment. It also examines the importance of integrating self-testing into existing health systems to support syphilis surveillance and prevention.

Read the article

Batac ALR, Marks M, Tucker JD, Peeling RW. Syphilis self-testing and implications for syphilis control and prevention. J Clin Microbiol. 2025 Dec 17;63(12):e0098225. doi: 10.1128/jcm.00982-25.
