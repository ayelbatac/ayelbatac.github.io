How I Found My Way to Public Health | Ayel L. R. Batac

A personal reflection on the experiences that led me to public health.

How I Found My Way to Public Health

May 7, 2025

\(~\)

When I started studying molecular biology as an undergraduate, I had a fairly ambitious goal. I wanted to help cure cancer.

I was fascinated by the human body and how much was happening at a level we couldn’t see. The more I learned about molecular processes, the more interested I became in what happens when they go wrong. Cancer had affected several members of my family, and I had lost relatives to it. I thought that if I could understand disease at its most fundamental level, I might one day contribute to finding a cure.

As my studies progressed, I began to appreciate just how difficult that ambition was. Cancer isn’t a single disease, and understanding its biology doesn’t necessarily mean we know how to treat it. Even when researchers make promising discoveries, it can take decades before those discoveries become treatments.

I developed an enormous respect for the patience and persistence that laboratory research demands. At the same time, I began questioning whether this was the kind of work I wanted to pursue. I was still interested in science, but I wasn’t entirely sure where I belonged within it.

That started to change when I joined the Children’s Hospital Research Institute of Manitoba as a research assistant. I’d encountered public health during my undergraduate studies, but most of my training had focused on the biological mechanisms of disease. Working on population health studies introduced me to a different side of medical research. I became interested in how people’s circumstances influence their health and why some communities experience greater difficulties accessing care.

I hadn’t expected to enjoy this kind of research as much as I did.

When COVID-19 arrived, I had the opportunity to work in public health in a different way. I joined the Government of Manitoba as an infectious disease epidemiologist, working alongside professionals involved in the provincial pandemic response.

The work was unlike anything I’d experienced before. We were interpreting data as the situation changed, often without all the information we needed. Decisions couldn’t always wait. Working alongside laboratory teams and medical officers of health, I experienced the urgency of a public health response in a way I hadn’t before.

I was also struck by how much coordination it took. Epidemiologists were only one part of the response. Clinicians and colleagues across government departments had important roles too, and we all depended on one another’s work.

I enjoyed the challenge of working through uncertainty, but what stayed with me most was how quickly our work was being put to use. In academic research, you can spend months on a project, publish the findings and move on without knowing how they’ve influenced practice. During the pandemic, the analyses we were doing were informing decisions as the situation unfolded.

I came away knowing that applied public health was something I wanted to pursue.

Over time, I became increasingly interested in HIV and other sexually transmitted infections.

Having studied molecular biology, I remembered learning about HIV’s formidable reverse transcriptase. It’s one reason the virus can change so quickly, making an effective vaccine particularly difficult to develop. Finding a cure is another challenge because HIV can remain hidden in the body, even with effective treatment. So I began thinking about HIV from a different angle. If a vaccine or cure remained out of reach, what could we do to prevent more people from acquiring it?

We already have effective tools to prevent transmission, and advances in treatment have transformed what it means to live with HIV. But many people still face difficulties accessing these services.

I found myself thinking more about the distance between what science has made possible and what people can realistically access.

My interest in sexual health is also personal. In the conservative Filipino communities I knew growing up, talking openly about sex was taboo. Even asking basic questions about sexual health could be difficult, despite how important those conversations were.

Seeing HIV cases rise in the Philippines, especially among young people, worries me. It makes me think about how much more we could do to make sexual health education accessible. How can you ask a question you don’t even know you need to ask? It bothers me that fear of judgement can still stop people from getting tested. No one should have to feel ashamed of looking after their health.

There’s another part of my childhood that has stayed with me.

When I was seven years old, I knew children my age who were being sexually exploited for money. I remember wanting to play with them, but sometimes they couldn’t, and I didn’t understand why. It was only as I grew older that I began to understand those moments, and how difficult life could be when there wasn’t even enough money for food. I was far too young to fully understand what was happening, but it was already part of the lives of children around me.

While I was able to enjoy being a child, they were facing circumstances no child should have to experience. They deserved so much more than what they had to go through, and they still do.

It’s difficult for me to think about health as a matter of individual choice alone. Not everyone has the same opportunities to look after their health, and I think we need to remember that when we talk about prevention.

At the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, I’ve been able to explore these questions through my research on HIV and STI prevention. I’m particularly interested in how people engage with health services and what can be learned from interventions designed to improve access to care.

My research has also made me more conscious of whose experiences we pay attention to. That can shape the questions we ask and what we learn from them.

I still value the scientific training that first drew me into molecular biology. What has changed is the kinds of questions I want to investigate and the ways I hope to contribute.

Academic research remains important to me, but I also want to make what I learn useful beyond academic publications. Writing has become one way for me to do that. That’s partly why I created this website. It gives me a place to share my research, reflect on what I’m learning and explore questions that I’m still working through.

I once imagined that my contribution to medicine would come from helping discover a cure. I still admire the scientists pursuing those discoveries, and I know how important their work is.

These days, I find myself asking a different question: how can we make better use of the knowledge and interventions we already have so that more people can benefit from them?

I don’t have all the answers. But I know that this is the kind of work I want to keep doing.

–Ayel
