Sexually Transmitted Infections Research | Ayel L. R. Batac

Research on sexually transmitted infections by Ayel L. R. Batac, focusing on public health, diagnostics, and knowledge translation in global contexts.

SEXUALLY TRANSMITTED INFECTIONS

\(~\)

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs), including gonorrhoea and chlamydia, remain a significant public health concern worldwide. Many STIs cause few or no symptoms and may go undiagnosed without timely testing. In many settings, men who have sex with men (MSM) experience a disproportionate burden of STIs, making regular access to testing particularly important. Stigma surrounding same-sex sexual behaviour and concerns about confidentiality can discourage engagement with STI services. Out-of-pocket costs may create further barriers, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where affordable testing is not consistently available.

My research examines how social and behavioural factors shape people’s engagement with STI prevention and their experiences of sexual health services. Using mixed methods, I investigate these relationships and consider how the evidence can inform the design and implementation of public health interventions.

Pay-It-Forward STI Testing

The Pay-It-Forward Strategy

The pay-it-forward strategy is a social innovation built on a simple idea: people can receive a health service free of charge and, if they wish, help someone else access the same service. In STI testing, this means offering free gonorrhoea and chlamydia tests while inviting recipients to contribute towards testing for others. Participation is entirely voluntary, and access to testing does not depend on making a contribution.

The approach reduces the immediate cost of testing while creating opportunities for people to support others who may face similar barriers to care:

  • Access: Reducing financial barriers to STI testing.
  • Reciprocity: Encouraging voluntary contributions that help others access testing.
  • Community participation: Creating opportunities for individuals to support STI prevention by recommending testing to peers.

Unlike conventional fee-for-service models, pay-it-forward introduces community support into the delivery of healthcare. The strategy may be particularly relevant in settings where financial constraints limit access to STI services, although stigma and concerns about confidentiality may still discourage people from seeking care.

Rethinking Community Participation in STI Prevention

STI testing programmes often measure their reach through the number of people who access services. Pay-it-forward introduces another dimension of participation, allowing individuals to contribute to the health of others rather than remaining solely recipients of care. This creates opportunities for communities to take a more active role in STI prevention, particularly through the social networks and relationships that connect people beyond conventional healthcare settings.

Meaningful participation, however, may take different forms depending on people’s circumstances. Individuals who wish to support others may still be reluctant to engage in activities that could reveal their sexual health concerns or expose them to stigma. Recognising these differences can help public health programmes create opportunities for community involvement that respect individual preferences and allow people to contribute in ways they feel comfortable.

Ongoing Research

A pragmatic cluster randomised controlled trial (RCT) conducted through community-based organisations and public health clinics in Guangdong Province, China, provides the basis for my ongoing mixed methods research on pay-it-forward gonorrhoea and chlamydia testing among MSM. The work examines the effectiveness and implementation of the strategy, with particular attention to how individuals engage with opportunities to support others.

Survey data provide information about patterns of participation, while qualitative interviews explore what motivates individuals to contribute and what may discourage them from doing so. The research focuses on:

  • STI testing uptake: Evaluating how pay-it-forward strategies influence the use of gonorrhoea and chlamydia testing services.
  • Prosocial behaviour and community participation: Investigating motivations for voluntary contributions and how community connectedness may shape engagement with the intervention.
  • Stigma and participation: Examining how concerns about others discovering an individual’s use of STI services or same-sex sexual behaviour may influence their willingness to participate.

Understanding these experiences can inform how pay-it-forward is delivered within existing sexual health services, particularly by accommodating different forms of participation while respecting privacy and individual choice.

Vaccination for STI Prevention

Meningococcal Vaccines and Gonorrhoea Prevention

The four-component meningococcal serogroup B (4CMenB) vaccine was originally developed to prevent meningococcal disease, a serious bacterial infection that can cause meningitis (inflammation of the lining around the brain and spinal cord) or bloodstream infections. The bacterium responsible for meningococcal disease is closely related to the one that causes gonorrhoea, an STI. Because these bacteria share certain proteins, researchers began investigating whether meningococcal B vaccines might also provide protection against gonorrhoea. Earlier studies found lower rates of gonorrhoea among people who had received these vaccines, suggesting possible partial protection, although the findings did not establish that vaccination itself caused the difference.

In August 2025, England introduced a targeted 4CMenB vaccination programme, primarily for MSM at increased risk of gonorrhoea, including those with a recent bacterial STI diagnosis or multiple recent sexual partners. The decision followed concerns about high gonorrhoea rates and growing antibiotic resistance, alongside evidence suggesting that even partial vaccine protection could have public health benefits. However, more recent clinical trials have not confirmed the protection suggested by earlier studies, leaving uncertainty about the vaccine’s effectiveness against gonorrhoea. Understanding how people interpret this evolving evidence is therefore important for communicating the potential benefits and limitations of vaccination as an STI prevention strategy.

Ongoing Research

My research examines how vaccination for gonorrhoea prevention is discussed in online communities, with particular attention to how people interpret emerging evidence and express attitudes towards its potential use in STI prevention.

The research uses a mixed methods approach to content analysis, focusing on:

  • Information needs and attitudes: Examining the questions people raise about vaccination and how they interpret its potential benefits and risks.
  • Vaccination experiences and access: Exploring reported experiences of obtaining vaccination and the barriers that may influence access.
  • Changes over time: Investigating how topics and expressed attitudes evolve alongside developments in scientific evidence and vaccination policy.

Understanding these perspectives may help identify gaps in public knowledge and inform how emerging STI prevention strategies are communicated. Findings will reflect the contributions analysed within these online communities rather than being treated as representative of wider population attitudes.

Contribution to Public Health

Making STI prevention services available does not guarantee that people will use them. Understanding how individuals engage with testing and respond to emerging preventive approaches can help inform public health practice. My research aims to contribute by:

  • Supporting earlier STI detection: Informing testing approaches that could improve access to timely diagnosis and treatment, particularly where financial barriers discourage testing.
  • Strengthening community participation: Helping sexual health services accommodate voluntary contributions while respecting privacy and avoiding pressure to participate.
  • Improving communication about STI vaccination: Identifying questions and concerns that public health messaging should address, particularly when evidence about vaccine effectiveness remains uncertain.
  • Informing implementation in practice: Providing evidence about the social and practical considerations that may influence how STI prevention interventions are introduced and delivered within existing health services.
